Quercus shumardii

Description

Oak Compendium · Placeholder: the species description from an earlier pass (not the final text).

Leaves

Lobes per side: usually 3–4, rarely as few as 2.

Leaves are usually overall more rounded, nearly ovate, widest towards the tip rather than the midsection, with a wide base. I think of the lobes as being more expanded distally, and they often overlap, with very tight sinuses (vs the wider ones of Q. coccinea). One source says as few as 5 lobes and another as many as 11; as with most things oak, 7–9 is the typical range rather than a hard limit.

Lobing is generally deeper than Q. rubra and can be very deep, but I used to think shallow sinuses were not possible and have since seen labeled arboretum specimens with some shallow, Q. rubra-like leaves. Like most any oak it can have outsized shade leaves.

The lower surface is glabrous other than sporadic axial domatia, never densely hairy (tomentose) like Q. macrocarpa. My rough conclusion, from one area and some iNat data, is that the domatia are darker than on Q. palustris. My gut says Q. shumardii when the secondary veins look raised on both surfaces. Fall color is usually yellowish.

Many of these leaf traits can be more misleading than helpful: I have seen ground covered in definite Q. shumardii leaves that met many of the criteria for Q. coccinea. I have not developed enough of a sense of them to definitively ID them from a leaf.

Buds

At maturity the buds are totally glabrous, angled (five-angled in cross-section, like a pentagon or hexagon), and a bit pointed. The scale edges are not ciliate but more frayed, giving them pale margins that from a distance can look ciliate, so without close-ups it is probably impossible to tell. There are no hairs apically. Buds that look pubescent rule out Q. shumardii. New spring growth is tricky!

Twigs

My rough conclusion, from one area and some iNat data, is that new stems are always green, vs sometimes red with Q. palustris.

Bark

One of my books says Q. shumardii has a smooth transition in the bark pattern where the major limbs join the main trunk, with no collars, while Q. coccinea shows a growth that breaks up the transition. I have been looking for this in the field and it seems reasonably accurate. It is more obvious in the field than in photos, and the positive case (smooth transition) is more obvious than the negative.

Acorns

The cap is saucer shaped like Q. rubra rather than turbinate. Acorns can get pretty big and look a lot like the classic "fat man in a beret" acorns of Q. rubra, so you have to be careful with acorns.

Form

Q. shumardii gets HUGE, especially down south: the biggest of all of our oaks AFAIK. Even here in Northern VA we have some big ones. The forest-grown ones, while not scoring as high on points for lack of crown spread, are some of the most impressive to me. It can have drooping lower branches and look a lot like Q. palustris at times.

Range

Its range does not extend much past southern PA, and it does not reach northeastern Massachusetts. Around Glen Rose, Texas, it overlaps with Q. buckleyi. It is a common street tree and frequent adventive.

Habitat

Wet. Q. shumardii tends towards lowlands and co-occurs with Q. palustris, Q. bicolor and other lowland species; it is not dry tolerant in the wild. Q. rubra and Q. shumardii would not typically occur in the same habitat unless planted. A wetter site could be Q. shumardii; a dryish or upland site (no regular flooding or standing water, or with Pinus, Q. falcata, Juniperus) points away from it, towards Q. coccinea or Q. rubra. Q. buckleyi is the tree of drier climates, as seen in the East to West transition from one to the other.

Identification

To confirm these to species you usually need a close-up of the terminal buds showing hairiness and shape; even then they can be ambiguous. Once the buds are mature (or there are acorns) it should be possible to ID it for sure. The great irony is that oaks are hardest to ID in the spring, and I struggle to say how hairy buds are when expanded. With a sapling you may have to wait for the buds to mature and to see the fall color. Where it grows wild with Q. palustris and a few Q. rubra, new spring leaves on real small trees are very confusing. Q. shumardii is likely grossly under-reported because it is tricky to ID without clear photos of the terminal buds. Habitat does not help much where it could have been planted, and nursery stock has a good chance of being mislabeled at the source.

Look-alikes

  • Q. coccinea: a wide variety of leaf shapes with a lot of overlap; a close-up of the buds is usually needed, though I have run into some in the field that are highly ambiguous. Quite red fall color points to Q. coccinea.
  • Q. rubra: can look nearly identical (especially shaded leaves). Q. rubra buds are round in cross-section, Q. shumardii and Q. coccinea angled; then size and hairiness usually separate them.
  • Q. buckleyi: very similar; the biggest obvious difference is the buds, totally glabrous in Q. shumardii and a bit hairy (especially at the tip) in Q. buckleyi.

Hybrids

Where Q. buckleyi and Q. shumardii overlap naturally in Texas there are lots of intermediates, Q. × sternbergii. Q. coccinea and Q. shumardii both readily hybridize with Q. rubra (and probably each other), and one of my books mentions a ton of mixing between Q. shumardii, Q. rubra and Q. velutina. On iNat I see Q. shumardii IDs with non-shumardii characteristics (no smooth transition, hairy buds) that otherwise look like Q. shumardii. They can be confounding.

The domatia are likely black because of the critters (mostly mites) living in them; as the season progresses frass and debris accumulate and darken them.
